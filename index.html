Teaching Two Robot Arms to Lay Up Carbon Fiber

WEIRD Lab, University of Washington · Robotics Researcher & Amazon Robotics Research Fellow · Jan 2026 – present

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

Laying carbon fiber into a mold is done by hand because success depends on contact forces a skilled technician feels but can't write down — press too hard and the ply distorts, too soft and it wrinkles. I'm building a two-armed robot that learns the motion from human demonstrations instead of from a spec: the right arm performs the layup, and the left arm carries a laser profilometer that scans the surface afterward and measures how badly it wrinkled. That measurement is both the grade for the policy and, increasingly, the reward signal it trains against.

  • My role: I lead the system end to end — I design and fabricate the end-effectors and tooling for both arms, collect the demonstrations, write the controller, train the policies, and evaluate them on the real hardware.
  • How it works: A UR5e on a vertical rail (7 DoF) carries an end-effector modeled on the hand tool a technician actually uses. I collected 500+ GELLO teleoperated demonstrations across varied tools, strip sizes, and mold shapes, and run them through ACT and Diffusion Policy (LeRobot, ROS2) under a custom impedance-style PD controller.
  • What I found: Diffusion Policy clearly beats ACT here, because there is more than one valid way to lay a strip down and a policy that regresses to the mean of two good strategies produces a third one that fails.
  • Current work: Rewriting the impedance controller to operate on the mold's curved surface rather than in world coordinates — separating tangential twist commands from normal-axis force, so the same policy transfers across mold geometries.

Debugging a Robotic Vision System in Production

Amazon Robotics · Robotics Data Scientist Intern · Sep – Dec 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

A robotic vision system in production was failing intermittently, and had been for about a month with no identified cause — too rare to reproduce on demand, too frequent to ignore, and direct debugging had already stalled. Rather than keep chasing individual traces, I treated the production logs as a statistics problem: not "what went wrong here" but "which conditions are the failures not independent of."

  • My role: Owned the investigation, then contributed to the monitoring meant to catch the next one.
  • How it works: Statistical independence testing over production-scale logs, plus consistency metrics between perception models — models observing the same scene are geometrically constrained, so their disagreement is a signal you get without labels.
  • Outcome: Root cause isolated in under two weeks, unblocking the engineering fix.
  • Note: Confidential work — described at a deliberately high level.

A Clinical Imaging Payload Where Timing Is Measured in Microseconds

uWAMIT — UW Molecular Imaging & Therapy Center, with GE HealthCare · Mechanical Design & Mechatronics Researcher · Jun 2024 – Mar 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

Photoacoustic imaging works by firing a laser into tissue and listening to the ultrasound it generates — which only produces an image if the laser pulse and the ultrasound acquisition are synchronized to within microseconds. GE HealthCare wanted this running on a commercial scanner they already sell. I owned the mechanical payload that carries the optics, and built the position-synchronized triggering that lets both modalities run at once — which the platform had no mechanism for — from blank CAD through a validated prototype.

  • My role: Full mechanical design lifecycle plus the timing subsystem — SolidWorks parts under coupled optical, thermal, and mechanical constraints, GD&T per ASME Y14.5, sub-mm tolerance stack-up, first-article inspection, and integration.
  • The hard part: Firing the laser at known angular positions on a stage spinning at 17,400 deg/s — a capability the platform simply didn't have. I built it by triggering off the encoder (Aerotech position-synchronized output) rather than a clock, plus the 1000 Hz acquisition path in Python over UART.
  • Outcome: Met clinical-grade image resolution targets without extending the timeline; co-authored publication at SPIE Photonics West 2025.

Redesigning a Robot End-Effector That Was Faking Its Own Defects

Boeing Advanced Research Center (BARC) · Mechanical Design Research Assistant · May 2023 – Jun 2024

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

Boeing inspects composite parts by running an ultrasonic probe over them on a robot arm. On curved parts, the scans kept showing defects that weren't physically there — which is expensive, because you can't ship a part you don't trust. I ran the signal analysis that showed the artifacts came from the probe's own geometry and water coupling, not the part, then redesigned the end-effector to remove the cause.

  • My role: Diagnosis through delivered hardware — I found the root causes, designed the replacement probe, coordinated fabrication, inspected first articles, and validated it by scan testing.
  • How it works: FFT analysis of the scan signal isolated two artifact sources; a novel two-curve probe geometry (SolidWorks, GD&T, FEA, CNC-machinable) holds standoff across surfaces whose radius varies continuously; a redesigned fixture fixed the water-coupling one. I also implemented Boustrophedon cell decomposition for robot scan coverage.
  • Outcome: Geometry-induced artifacts eliminated; 40% better water retention, improving defect-detection reliability and SNR.

Building an Automated Test Machine From Scratch, Alone

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

I built the machine that replaced that process: the mechanical fixturing, the sensor and actuator integration, and the control software, from an empty bench to a system that runs unattended overnight.

  • Outcome: Every manual step eliminated, enabling continuous unattended operation; folded into the existing PLM/BOM workflow so it outlived my internship.

Closing the Loop on a Self-Driving Materials Lab

UW Sun Lab · Systems Engineering & Machine Learning Research Assistant · Sep 2024 – Apr 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

A self-driving lab is supposed to run experiments without a human: pick the next formulation, mix it, deposit it, measure the result, repeat. This one could do everything except the measuring — a person still had to eyeball sample concentrations, which meant the "autonomous" loop stopped every cycle and waited. I closed it with a camera.

  • My role: Built and deployed the perception half of the autonomy loop, and worked with the chemists to get it trusted enough to actually be used.
  • How it works: Real-time computer vision extracting concentration from sample color, feeding the deposition controller directly; Latin Hypercube Sampling over a 96-sample Opentrons plate for experiment design; handling for cross-contamination, lighting noise, and stock depletion.
  • Outcome: Replaced manual spot-checks with continuous automated feedback and was adopted into active deposition workflows.

A Tunnel Boring Machine, 45 Engineers, and a Fixed Deadline

Washington Hyperloop · Executive Director & Electromechanical Systems Engineer · Jan 2024 – Apr 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

A student team had to design and build a working tunnel boring machine from nothing and run it at The Boring Company's national competition on a date that would not move. I ran the organization and built its control system — which meant I was both the person setting the ME/EE/firmware schedule and the person up at night making the motors and pumps actually talk to the server.

  • My role: Executive Director over 5 subteams and 45+ engineers on a $20K budget, and hands-on owner of the electromechanical control system.
  • How it works: ESP32 firmware (Embedded C) driving motor drivers and pump controllers over UART/I2C, reporting to a Python data server for live telemetry; emergency-stop and manual-start paths designed for low-latency, fail-safe response.
  • Outcome: Zero control-system failures at competition, passed every mechanical, electrical, and software inspection, won the Cutterhead Mini-Competition, and came in under budget.
