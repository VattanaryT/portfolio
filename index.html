Robot Learning with Bimanual Arms for Carbon Fiber Layup Automation

WEIRD Lab, University of Washington · Robot Learning Researcher · Jan 2026 – present

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

Carbon fiber layup is still done by hand because success depends on precise contact forces of a skilled technician. Changes in pressure could lead to distortion and wrinkles. My team is using a bimanual robot that learns the motion from human demonstrations: the right arm performs the layup, and the left arm carries a laser profilometer that scans the surface afterward and measures the surface quality. That measurement is both the grade for the policy and, increasingly, the reward signal it trains against.

  • My role: I lead the system end to end, overseeing end-effector and tooling design for both arms, collecting the demonstrations on a GELLO-style teleoperation setup, writing the controller, training the policies, and evaluating them on the real hardware.
  • The pipeline: A config-driven three-stage pipeline (rosbag → trajectory pkl → LeRobot dataset → training) with sharded conversion and checkpoint resume, so one command reproduces any dataset or training run. I own the pytest infrastructure for both stacks (37 modules, 165 tests) with hardware/CI markers, so control-law and kinematics logic is verified without a robot in the loop.
  • How it works: A UR5e on a vertical rail (7 DoF) carries an end-effector modeled on the hand tool a technician actually uses. I collected 500+ GELLO teleoperated demonstrations across varied tools, strip sizes, and mold shapes, and train ACT and Diffusion Policy in PyTorch/CUDA, ablating 1D U-Net against transformer backbones across prediction and action horizons via Weights & Biases sweeps.
  • On hardware: Trained policies run closed-loop at 10 Hz under ROS2 Humble, with rollouts evaluated against a laser-profilometer scan of the workpiece captured by the second arm. I fused 6-DoF end-effector pose with wrist force/torque in the observation space and built an in-contact force loop on the real UR5e F/T over RTDE, plus DAgger-style human-in-the-loop intervention for targeted on-policy data collection against observed failure modes.
  • What I found: Diffusion Policy clearly beats ACT here, because there is more than one valid way to lay a strip down.
  • Current work: Rewriting the impedance controller to operate on the mold's curved surface rather than in world coordinates; separating tangential twist commands from normal-axis force, so the same policy transfers across mold geometries.

Debugging a Robotic Vision System in Production

Amazon Robotics · Robotics Data Scientist Intern · Sep – Dec 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

A robotic vision system in production needs to be further optimized. There were some parts that were failing intermittently, and had been for about a month with no identified cause — too rare to reproduce on demand, too frequent to ignore, and direct debugging had already stalled. Rather than keep chasing individual traces, I treated the production logs as a statistics problem: not "what went wrong here" but "which conditions are the failures not independent of."

  • My role: Owned the investigation, then contributed to the monitoring meant to catch the next one.
  • How it works: Chi-square and independence tests across millions of production cycles, plus cross-model consistency metrics for the segmentation, depth, and dimension pipelines. Models observing the same scene are geometrically constrained, so their disagreement is a signal you get without labels.
  • Outcome: Root cause confirmed in under two weeks, unblocking the engineering fix; the scalable Python pipelines over AWS S3 and SQL stayed behind for production monitoring and alerting.
  • Note: Confidential work — described at a deliberately high level.

Building an Automated Test Machine From Scratch, Alone

Powerlight Technologies · Automation Engineering Intern · Jun – Sep 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

Testing photovoltaic cells was entirely manual, so throughput was capped by how long a person could stand at the bench — and the data drifted depending on who ran it. I built the machine that replaced that process: the mechanical fixturing, the sensor and actuator integration, and the control software, from an empty bench to a system that runs unattended overnight.

  • My role: Sole engineer, scoping through commissioning — hardware selection, design, build, software, and documentation.
  • How it works: SolidWorks fixtures and assembly tooling holding sub-0.1 mm optical alignment; integrated high-speed cameras, an InGaAs spectrometer, DAQ, and actuators; Python control software with a PyQt operator GUI; full BOM and V&V documentation.
  • Outcome: Every manual step eliminated, enabling continuous unattended operation; folded into the existing PLM/BOM workflow so it outlived my internship.

Closing the Loop on a Self-Driving Materials Lab

UW Sun Lab · Systems Engineering & Machine Learning Research Assistant · Amazon Robotics Research Fellow · Sep 2024 – Apr 2025

HardwareSensingControlLearningDeployment

A self-driving lab is supposed to run experiments without a human: pick the next formulation, mix it, deposit it, measure the result, repeat. This one could do everything except the measuring — a person still had to eyeball sample concentrations, which meant the "autonomous" loop stopped every cycle and waited. I closed it with a camera.

  • My role: Built and deployed the perception half of the autonomy loop, and worked with the chemists to get it trusted enough to actually be used.
  • How it works: Real-time computer vision extracting concentration from sample color, feeding the deposition controller directly; Latin Hypercube Sampling over a 96-sample Opentrons plate for experiment design; handling for cross-contamination, lighting noise, and stock depletion.
  • Outcome: Replaced manual spot-checks with continuous automated feedback and was adopted into active deposition workflows.
