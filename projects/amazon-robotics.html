Debugging a Robotic Vision System in Production

Amazon Robotics · Robotics Data Scientist Intern · Sep – Dec 2025

This work is confidential. What follows is a high-level description of the problem shape and my approach — no system details, data, or results.

The problem shape

A robotic vision system in production was failing intermittently, and had been for about a month without an identified cause. Failures like this are genuinely hard for a structural reason: they are too rare to reproduce on demand, so you cannot attach a debugger and wait for one, but too frequent to write off as noise. Direct debugging had already been attempted and had not converged.

My approach

My contribution was to reframe it. The system had already recorded the evidence — every cycle it ran was logged with its conditions and its outcome. That makes it a hypothesis-testing problem rather than a debugging problem, and it changes the question from "what went wrong in this trace" to "which conditions are the failures not independent of."

I applied statistical independence testing across the logged population to find conditions whose association with failure was too strong to be chance. The useful inversion is that at production scale, the same rarity that makes a failure impossible to catch by hand is what makes it visible statistically — you stop trying to catch one instance and start measuring the population.

I also worked on consistency metrics between perception models. The principle is that models observing the same physical scene through different representations are mutually constrained by geometry, so their disagreement is measurable without ground-truth labels. When they disagree beyond what their independent error rates explain, at least one is wrong — and you learn that continuously, in production, without anyone annotating anything. I built the supporting data pipelines in Python over AWS S3 and SQL.

Outcome

  • Root cause isolated in under two weeks, substantially faster than the preceding effort, which unblocked the engineering fix.
  • The consistency work fed into ongoing production monitoring, so this class of failure is caught rather than rediscovered.

Why this matters for deployed robots

A learned policy that works in the lab and degrades quietly in the field is the default failure mode of robot deployment, and it is rarely a dramatic crash — it is a slow drift in a perception input that nothing was watching. The transferable lesson is that monitoring has to be designed alongside the system, and that models observing the same physical world can be made to check each other for free.
