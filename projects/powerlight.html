Building an Automated Test Machine From Scratch, Alone

Powerlight Technologies · Automation Engineering Intern · Jun – Sep 2025

The problem

Powerlight transmits power optically — a laser to a photovoltaic receiver — so the performance of individual PV cells is a core product parameter, and characterizing them is a constant activity rather than a one-time qualification.

That characterization was fully manual. Two things follow from that, and the second is worse than the first. Throughput is capped by how long a person is willing to stand at a bench placing cells and triggering measurements. And the data drifts: alignment done by hand varies between operators and across a long session, so measurements from different days are not strictly comparable. You end up unable to tell a real change in cell performance from a change in who ran the test.

Scope

I was the only engineer on it, from scoping through commissioning: hardware selection, mechanical design, sensor and actuator integration, control software, and the documentation needed for anyone else to use and maintain it.

Mechanical SolidWorks fixtures and assembly tooling holding sub-0.1 mm optical alignment, repeatably, without operator judgment.
Sensing High-speed cameras and an InGaAs spectrometer for characterization, integrated with DAQ.
Actuation Motion and positioning to sequence cells through the station unattended.
Software Python control layer coordinating motion, acquisition, and logging; PyQt GUI for operators.
Documentation Full BOM and V&V records; integrated into existing PLM/BOM workflows.

Why the fixture was the hard part

The sub-0.1 mm alignment requirement is set by the physics: the measurement is optical, so a small positional error changes the incident geometry and therefore the reading. In manual operation that error was being absorbed by a human eyeballing each placement — which is exactly the source of drift.

Automating the measurement without solving the fixturing would have produced a machine that generates bad data faster. So the design goal was to move the alignment tolerance out of the operator's hands and into the fixture geometry, where it is repeatable and can be verified once rather than trusted every cycle.

Outcome

  • Every manual step eliminated, enabling continuous unattended operation — the machine runs overnight.
  • Measurement consistency no longer depends on the operator, so results are comparable across sessions.
  • Integrated into the company's existing PLM and BOM workflows, so it stayed in use after the internship ended rather than becoming an orphaned intern project.
Why this is on a robotics portfolio: it is the same loop as a robot cell, minus the arm — mechanical design, sensing, actuation, and control software, owned by one person and commissioned into production use. The habit it builds is designing the fixture and the software as one system rather than two.
