Work Resume

A Clinical Imaging Payload Where Timing Is Measured in Microseconds

uWAMIT — UW Molecular Imaging & Therapy Center, with GE HealthCare · Mechanical Design & Mechatronics Researcher · Jun 2024 – Mar 2025

What photoacoustic imaging demands of the hardware

Photoacoustic imaging fires a nanosecond laser pulse into tissue. The tissue absorbs it, heats by a fraction of a degree, expands, and emits an ultrasound wave. Read that wave with an ultrasound probe and you get an image whose contrast comes from optical absorption — so you can see things like blood oxygenation that pure ultrasound cannot show.

The catch is that the image only reconstructs if you know precisely when the laser fired relative to when the ultrasound array started listening. Sound travels roughly 1.5 mm per microsecond in tissue, so a microsecond of timing error is a millimeter of spatial error. Timing accuracy is not a performance nicety here; it is the resolution of the instrument.

The project's goal made this harder: rather than build a bespoke research rig, GE HealthCare wanted this modality running on a commercial scanner they already sell. That meant designing a payload that fits an existing product's mechanical and electrical envelope, and achieving clinical-grade validation rather than a working demo.

Mechanical design under coupled constraints

I owned the full mechanical design lifecycle of the payload. The design problem is that the optical, thermal, and mechanical requirements are not separable:

  • Optical — fiber output and the ultrasound array must hold alignment, because relative position between illumination and reception is baked into the reconstruction.
  • Thermal — laser delivery deposits heat, and thermal expansion moves the very geometry the optics depend on.
  • Mechanical — the assembly is handheld and scanning, so it takes real loads while holding sub-millimeter relationships.

Work included SolidWorks parts and assemblies, GD&T per ASME Y14.5, sub-millimeter tolerance stack-up analysis, first-article inspection of fabricated parts, and full subsystem integration — plus the AC/DC power supply enclosure and cable management that make the thing usable in a clinical setting rather than a lab bench.

Tolerance stack-up mattered more than usual: alignment is enforced by the accumulated tolerances of the parts between the fiber and the array, so the stack-up analysis is the image quality budget.

The synchronization failure at 3000 RPM

The system uses a rotating stage to sweep illumination. At 3000 RPM (17,400 deg/s), laser-fiber synchronization failed and image resolution degraded.

Faults like this are expensive because the symptom appears in the image, which is the last stage of a chain that crosses three engineering domains. Mechanical runout, electrical trigger latency, and software timing jitter all produce the same blur. I isolated it by testing the layers separately rather than guessing — characterizing each independently so the domain could be identified before anything was redesigned.

Position-synchronized triggering

The fix was to stop triggering the laser on time and start triggering it on position. Using the Aerotech stage's Position Synchronized Output, the controller fires pulses off the encoder itself, so a pulse is emitted when the stage physically reaches an angle rather than when a timer expires.

This removes the entire error path: velocity ripple, control-loop lag, and host-side scheduling jitter no longer displace the pulse, because the encoder is the source of truth for both the firing and the reconstruction. I implemented the trigger configuration — total fixed time, ON time, and pulse count — and the Python control layer over UART, driving a 1000 Hz multimodal acquisition (fiber optics, laser timing, ultrasound) with custom Automation1 scripts, plus the Python API and operator GUI for the full workflow.

The general lesson: when a timing requirement is really a geometric requirement, derive the trigger from the position sensor instead of the clock. The same reasoning applies to camera-to-actuator synchronization in any robot that has to act on what it saw at a specific pose.

Outcome

  • Concept through validated prototype, meeting clinical-grade image resolution targets — without extending the project timeline.
  • Structured V&V and first-article inspection carried through to a system integrated on a commercial scanner.
