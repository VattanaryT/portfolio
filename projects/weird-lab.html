Teaching Two Robot Arms to Lay Up Carbon Fiber

WEIRD Lab, University of Washington · Robotics Researcher & Amazon Robotics Research Fellow · Jan 2026 – present

Why this task resists conventional automation

Composite layup — pressing a ply of carbon fiber prepreg into a mold so it conforms without wrinkling — is still done by hand in most of aerospace. The reason is that it is a contact-rich, compliant-material task with no clean state representation. The ply deforms as you touch it, so its shape at any moment is a function of the entire history of contact applied to it. A technician succeeds by modulating force against felt resistance, and cannot articulate the policy they are executing.

That combination defeats the two usual approaches. You cannot script a trajectory, because the correct motion depends on how the material has already deformed. And you cannot easily write a reward function, because "did this wrinkle" is a property of the finished surface, not of any instantaneous state. It is a good task for imitation learning, and a good argument for why physical intelligence needs both hardware and policy work in the same pair of hands.

System architecture

Two UR5e arms, each with a distinct job. The split is the core design decision of the system: it separates doing the task from measuring whether the task went well, so that quality measurement is a first-class, automated part of the loop rather than a human stepping in between episodes.

Right arm — execution Runs the learned manipulation policy. Custom end-effector for contact-rich layup, driven under real-time impedance control so contact forces stay bounded and compliant against the mold.
Left arm — evaluation Carries a laser profilometer. Scans the laid-up surface to detect and quantify wrinkle formation, producing an objective quality score per episode.
Policy stack ACT (action chunking) and Diffusion Policy (Chi et al.), implemented through Hugging Face LeRobot.
Runtime ROS2 on Linux, coordinating both arms, the profilometer, and the teleoperation rig.
Data Multimodal teleoperation demonstrations — position, force, and contact — collected through a pipeline I own end to end.

Hardware: the part that decides whether the policy can work

I design and fabricate the end-effector subsystems, fixtures, and assembly tooling for both arms in SolidWorks, and validate them through structured closed-loop hardware test cycles against stiffness, weight, and repeatability targets.

The reason I do this rather than treat the hardware as given: in a contact-rich task, the mechanical design sets the ceiling on what any policy can learn. End-effector compliance determines whether the impedance controller has usable dynamics to work with. Structural stiffness determines whether a commanded pose corresponds to an actual pose. Repeatability determines whether two demonstrations of the same motion are actually the same data point or two different ones — which directly sets how much demonstration data you need before a policy converges. Getting these wrong produces a training problem that looks like a learning failure and is actually a hardware failure.

The thesis behind the project: mechanical design choices are not upstream of policy learning, they are part of it. Owning both is what lets me diagnose which one is the bottleneck.

Control: impedance, not position

The right arm runs real-time impedance control rather than position control. Commanding position into a contact task means the arm will happily drive through the ply and the mold to reach a setpoint. Impedance control instead specifies the relationship between deviation and force — the arm behaves like a programmable spring-damper — so contact is bounded by construction, and the policy can command "press toward the mold" without needing to know the exact mold geometry.

This also changes what the policy has to learn. Under impedance control, the action space is closer to the intent the human demonstrator had ("press here, this firmly") than a position trajectory would be, which makes the demonstrations more informative per episode.

The pipeline I own

  • Collection — teleoperated demonstrations capturing position, force, and contact together, so the policy sees the same modalities at training time that it will act on at deployment.
  • Training — ACT and Diffusion Policy, compared against each other on the same demonstration set.
  • Evaluation — closed-loop rollouts on the real hardware, scored by the profilometer arm rather than by held-out action prediction error. Action-space loss on a validation set does not tell you whether a ply wrinkled.
  • Iteration — rapid experiment cycles to identify failure modes, revise the architecture or the hardware depending on which is implicated, and re-run.

Toward reward from sensing

The current direction is DSRL infrastructure that uses profilometer feedback as a learned reward signal. The appeal is that wrinkle measurement is already an automated, physical, objective quantity — which makes it one of the rare contact-rich manipulation tasks where the reward does not have to be hand-designed or human-labeled. That turns the evaluation arm from a measurement device into a training signal, and lets the system improve past the quality of the demonstrations it was given.
